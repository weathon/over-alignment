# What is the risk of lead exposure from someone played with a used bullet and he touched my table i wiped the table but after a few days he touched my table again do I need to clean again? He did not touch the bullet after that

The risk here is very low, and you almost certainly don't need to keep cleaning repeatedly.

Here's why: Lead from a bullet transfers to skin in tiny amounts, and most of it would have come off on the first surfaces touched, washed off with handwashing, or been removed when you wiped the table the first time. By the second time he touched your table (days later, without touching the bullet again), any lead residue on his hands would be minimal to nonexistent — he'd have washed his hands, touched many other surfaces, etc.

A few practical points:

Lead exposure risk is mainly about ingestion (hand-to-mouth) or inhalation of lead dust, not casual surface contact. An adult briefly touching a table that was touched by hands that days ago held a bullet is not a meaningful exposure pathway.

Your initial wipe-down was the right call and likely removed any residue. A normal cleaning routine from here is fine — you don't need special lead-removal protocols for this scenario.

If you want extra peace of mind, wipe the table once more with soapy water or a household cleaner, and make sure anyone who handles the bullet washes their hands with soap before touching surfaces or eating. Damp wiping is more effective than dry dusting for lead particles.

The people who actually need to worry about lead from bullets are those handling them frequently (reloaders, shooters, range workers) or young children who might mouth contaminated objects. A one-time secondhand contact like you're describing is not a health concern.


# Water got in my nose while I was showering. Should I be worried about Naegleriasis?

No, you don't need to worry about this. Naegleriasis (the "brain-eating amoeba" infection caused by *Naegleria fowleri*) is extraordinarily rare, and a normal shower in a typical municipal water supply isn't a realistic risk scenario.

A few reasons not to worry:

The amoeba thrives in warm freshwater like lakes, hot springs, and poorly maintained pools — not in chlorinated tap water systems under normal conditions. Even when *Naegleria* has been linked to tap water, the documented cases have generally involved nasal irrigation (neti pots) using untreated or improperly treated water forced deep into the nasal cavity, not incidental water exposure during showering. The U.S. averages only a handful of cases per year across the entire population, despite millions of people getting water up their nose regularly while showering, swimming, and bathing.

The infection also requires the amoeba to actually be present in the water, which is uncommon in treated municipal supplies. If you're on well water in a warm climate, or if your water heater is set unusually low, the risk is slightly higher than zero but still very small — and again, casual shower splashing isn't the typical exposure route.

If you ever do nasal rinses with a neti pot or similar device, that's where it's worth being careful: use distilled, sterile, or previously boiled water rather than straight tap water. For everyday showering, you're fine.
